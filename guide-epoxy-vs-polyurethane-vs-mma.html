Epoxy vs polyurethane vs MMA flooring: which do you need?

“Resin flooring” covers three main families: epoxy, polyurethane (PU) and MMA. They look similar once they’re down, but they behave very differently under heat, chemicals, cold and time pressure. Picking the wrong one is the most common reason a resin floor fails early. Here’s how to choose.

Epoxy: the all-rounder

Epoxy is the most common resin floor, and for most dry industrial and commercial areas it’s the right answer. It bonds very strongly to properly prepared concrete, it’s hard-wearing, it resists oils and most cleaning chemicals, and it gives a smooth, bright, easy-clean finish.

It comes in different builds:

  • Coatings (0.3–1 mm): stores, plant rooms, light workshops, with anti-slip aggregate where needed
  • Self-levelling (2–3 mm): heavy traffic, smooth seamless finish, labs and clean areas
  • Decorative flake and quartz: showrooms, offices, changing rooms and stairs

Where epoxy struggles: hot water, steam cleaning and big temperature swings. Epoxy is rigid, so repeated thermal shock can crack it or make it lift. It can also yellow in direct sunlight. In those areas, PU is the better choice.

Polyurethane: for heat, water and food

Polyurethane floors, especially PU screeds (often called “PU concrete” or “PU cement”), are built for the harshest areas: food and drink production, dairies, breweries, meat and fish processing, and commercial kitchens.

  • They handle hot washdowns, steam cleaning and spills of boiling water without cracking
  • They resist food acids, fats, sugars, blood and strong cleaning chemicals that slowly eat into other floors
  • They’re slightly flexible, so they cope with impact and thermal shock
  • They can be laid with a textured anti-slip finish for wet floors, and with coving and falls to drains

PU costs more than epoxy, but in a wet or hot process area it will far outlast it. See our food production flooring guide for the details.

MMA: when you can’t close for long

MMA (methyl methacrylate) resin cures in about an hour, even in freezing conditions. That makes it the go-to system when downtime is the problem: a supermarket aisle overnight, a production area over a weekend, a cold store that can’t be warmed up, or a car park deck that has to reopen the next morning.

The trade-offs: MMA costs more per m² and has a strong smell while it’s being laid. The building needs good ventilation, and it usually isn’t laid near open food or in occupied areas. The smell goes once it has cured.

Frequently asked questions

Is polyurethane better than epoxy?

Not better, just different. PU is the better choice where there’s hot water, steam cleaning, food acids or big temperature changes. In dry warehouses and workshops, epoxy does the job well and costs less.

Which resin floor is cheapest?

An epoxy roller coating is usually the cheapest resin floor. Self-levelling epoxy and flake finishes cost more, PU screeds more again, and MMA is usually the most expensive per m². The cheapest system isn’t always the cheapest over the life of the floor, though.

Can MMA be laid in winter?

Yes. MMA cures well even below freezing, which is one of its main advantages. Epoxy and PU need the floor and air to be around 10 °C or warmer, so in winter the building may need heating during the install.

Does MMA flooring smell?

Yes, MMA has a strong smell while it’s being laid and curing. We plan ventilation and timing around it, and it’s usually not used near open food. Once cured, the floor has no smell.

Can you lay a new resin floor over an old one?

Often, yes, if the old floor is still well bonded. We test it, prepare the surface and choose a compatible system. If the old floor is lifting or failing, it has to come off back to sound concrete first.
