How long does resin flooring take to install and cure?

Downtime is often the biggest worry with a new floor. The good news is that most resin floors are laid in a few days, and there are ways to cut that further. Here’s what a typical job looks like, when you can use the floor again, and what you can do to keep your site running.

By Casian IRF, industrial resin flooring contractors · Updated November 2026

What happens on a typical job

  1. Survey and quote. We look at the floor, check for moisture, old coatings and damage, and agree the system and programme.
  2. Preparation. Shot-blasting or diamond grinding to open up the concrete, with dust extraction. A crew can usually prepare a few hundred m² a day on an open floor.
  3. Repairs. Cracks, holes and joints are filled with resin mortar.
  4. Primer. Seals the concrete and gives the resin something to grip. Usually left to cure overnight.
  5. Body coats or screed. One or more layers, with anti-slip aggregate if needed. Epoxy and PU usually need overnight curing between coats.
  6. Topcoat, coving and line marking. The final seal coat, then any walkway or bay markings.

For a straightforward coating on an open warehouse floor, that’s typically 2–4 days on site. Thicker systems, lots of small rooms or a lot of repairs take longer.

What slows things down

Cold

Epoxy and PU need the floor and air to be at least about 10 °C to cure properly, and they cure slowly in the cold. An unheated warehouse in January may need heaters running during the install. MMA cures well even below freezing, so it’s often the answer for cold stores and unheated buildings.

Damp concrete

Resin needs a dry slab. New concrete normally needs several weeks to dry out first. As a rough guide, a 100 mm slab needs at least a month in good conditions, and longer in winter. We test the moisture level before laying. If the slab is too damp, a moisture-tolerant primer or damp-proof membrane can be used instead of waiting.

The state of the floor

Old coatings to remove, lots of cracks, uneven floors that need levelling, and oil-soaked concrete all add time before the resin can go down.

Small rooms and detail

Toilets, changing rooms, stairs and coving are slow, careful work. 100 m² spread over ten small rooms takes much longer than 100 m² in one open area.

How to keep downtime short

  • Phase the work so part of the site stays open while each area is done in turn.
  • Use weekends, nights or planned shutdowns. Tell us early so we can plan the crew around them.
  • Choose a fast-cure system like MMA where every hour of downtime costs money. See epoxy vs PU vs MMA.
  • Have the area cleared of stock, racking and equipment before we arrive.
  • Sort power, water and access in advance, and keep other trades off the floor while it cures.
  • Heat the building in winter if using epoxy or PU.

Frequently asked questions

Can a resin floor be done over a weekend?

Often, yes. Smaller areas can be done with MMA over a single night or weekend and be back in use straight after. Larger epoxy or PU floors can be phased over several weekends so you never have to close.

Do we have to close the whole building?

Usually not. We can sheet off and do one area at a time. Shot-blasting and grinding are dusty and noisy, but we use dust extraction and can plan the noisiest work for when you’re quietest.

Can resin flooring be laid in winter?

Yes. Epoxy and PU need the building to be at least about 10 °C, so we may need heaters running. MMA cures in freezing conditions, so it’s a good option for cold stores and unheated buildings.

How soon after pouring new concrete can resin go down?

New concrete has to dry out first. That usually means at least a month for a typical slab, longer in winter or for thick slabs. We test moisture before laying. If you can’t wait, a moisture-tolerant primer or membrane may be an option.

Does resin flooring smell?

Epoxy and PU have a low smell. MMA has a strong smell while it’s being laid and curing, so it needs good ventilation. All of them are odour-free once fully cured.
